Nobody serious still argues that the junior engineer is having a great time with wide range of AI tools. What almost nobody has done is take the alternative explanation seriously: 

Basic skill was never taught. It was absorbed from cheap failure.

That distinction matters, because it predicts something the current discourse does not. If basic skill were taught, then the disappearance of junior roles would be a pedagogical problem with an educational fix. If basic skill is absorbed from cheap failure, then the disappearance of junior roles is not a problem at all. It is the removal of the cheapest available teacher.

![The apprenticeship loop that nobody designed. Step 2 is the one disappearing.](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HUHM1XnacAAnOd9.png)

Start with the profession that has the strongest claim to having solved this. American residency training is, by a wide margin, the most expensive deliberately-protected apprenticeship in the US economy. Eighty-hour weekly caps, enforced since 2003. Mandatory handoffs. Attending sign-off on everything. The whole edifice exists for one declared reason: a resident who has never been allowed to make a serious mistake unsupervised does not yet know how to make one supervised either¹.

In September 2026, the ACGME proposed a set of changes to those Common Program Requirements. The 80-hour cap stays. But the requirement to provide eight hours off between shifts, which had been a citable Detail-level requirement, gets deleted outright, because, in the document's own words, the 80-hour average "is a deterrent for scheduling fewer than eight hours off." Post-24-hour-call rest drops from 14 hours to 12².

Read that again, because it is the whole argument in miniature. Nobody had to argue that junior work is inefficient. The inefficiency was already conceded. All that was needed was for the constraints protecting the training to become formally unenforceable, and for a workforce planner somewhere to notice.

![Tech entry-level share (blue, bottom) thins steadily from 2019 to 2025 while senior (gold) thickens; healthcare and retail entry shares stay broadly flat. Source: Indeed Hiring Lab | Sources [7]](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HUHNKtkakAE3Vej.jpg)

The people most exposed to that erosion are the ones who will staff the attending rooms in 2045. In the AMA's 2026 Physician Survey on Augmented Intelligence, 81% of physicians reported using AI in their practice, up from 38% three years earlier³. And in the same survey, 88% expressed concern about potential skill loss, concentrated among physicians with 10 years or less in practice³.

That is a profession with the strongest apprenticeship protection in the advanced economy, staffed by people who mostly think the apprenticeship is already leaking. The randomized evidence supports their worry without settling it: in the only controlled trial of ambient documentation scribes, 238 outpatient physicians across 14 specialties showed a 9.5% reduction in time-in-note with one vendor and no significant effect with another⁴. Independently, researchers validating AI-generated notes found a hallucination rate of 31% against 20% for physician-authored notes⁵.

Nine and a half percent is not a revolution. It is enough.

Now apply the same question to software, where the protection is nonexistent by default. In July 2026, Indeed Hiring Lab reported that US software development job postings have risen almost 15% since Claude Code launched in late February 2025, while total postings across all occupations fell 7%⁶. Read that alone and the sector is booming.

![Software development postings rise to 114.6 while all US postings fall to 93. Source: Indeed Hiring Lab | Sources [6]](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HUHNhAnbwAAmkeA.jpg)

Read the next sentence. Of the net increase in software postings between May 2025 and May 2026, 71% came from senior roles and 37% from titles mentioning AI, with the two categories overlapping⁶. In the first quarter of 2026, seniors accounted for 69.3% of software development postings while entry-level accounted for 4.5%, the lowest entry-level share of any sector Indeed examines⁷.

Not 4.5% fewer entry-level jobs. Four and a half percent of postings. The market for the first rung is now a rounding error.

There is an honest problem with that number, and it should be stated plainly. Indeed recently changed how it categorizes seniority, moving from titles and stated years of experience to classifying the actual responsibilities and training a role requires⁷. 

And the shift toward senior looks substantially like mid-level roles being squeezed out rather than entry-level roles being eliminated: between 2019 and 2025 the entry-level share fell barely a percentage point while the mid-level share fell 7.7 points⁷. Take the measurement caveat seriously.

But the direction survives it. Entry-level postings fell 7.5% year over year while senior rose 14.7%⁷. Whatever the categories, the base of the ladder is thinning.

The payroll data says the same thing in harder language. Using high-frequency ADP records through June 2026, the Stanford Digital Economy Lab found that employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations sits 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers, while experienced workers show no comparable gap⁸. Note what that is: a relative shortfall against a counterfactual, not a 19% collapse. In absolute terms, the most-exposed quintile of 22 to 25 year olds is down roughly 11% since late 2022, while the least-exposed groups rose roughly 10%⁸.

The mechanism is the part that should worry anyone building a career on this. The gap opens through reduced hiring, not through layoffs⁸. Layoffs are visible, reversible, and generate lawsuits. A hiring freeze is invisible and permanent. Firms that adopt generative AI stop opening the door, and the cohort that would have walked through it arrives to find the door welded shut.

And here the strongest objection deserves to be stated rather than buried. In September 2026, Robert Fairlie and Jane Wu published the first serious estimates of AI's effect on the unemployment of recent college graduates, using CPS microdata through August 2026. Their finding: unemployment did not spike in summer 2026 relative to previous summers, and did not rise significantly relative to older graduates⁹. Recent-graduate unemployment averaged 7.3% in summer 2026, against 7.1% in 2022, 6.3% in 2023, 7.8% in 2024, and 7.2% in 2025. Under an expanded definition that counts people who say they want a job, the rate was 10.4%, the highest of five summers, but only 0.3 points above the previous high⁹.

That is a genuinely strong result and it deserves an honest reply rather than a dismissal. The reconciliation is definitional, and the definition is the whole mechanism. ADP counts people on payroll. CPS counts people who are looking. Someone who was never hired is not unemployed; they were never in the labor force to begin with. The two datasets are not measuring contradictory realities. They are measuring the population that got in and the population that did not.

![Two readings of one dataset. Slots held; density of cheap failure per slot fell.](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HUHOVqAasAERE23.png)

The same result appears in medicine, and it is why the analogy is worth extending. NRMP reported a 2026 match that was the largest in its history: 48,050 active applicants, 44,344 positions offered, a 93.5% fill rate, and a 93.5% match rate for US medical school seniors, flat since 2024¹⁰. Residency slots are not disappearing. They are getting harder to get into.

That is precisely the pattern worth naming. The opportunity count is holding. The density of cheap failure inside each position is collapsing.

Where the work went is not mysterious. The Wall Street Journal reported that Handshake internship postings fell 16% year over year, with an average of 109 applicants per internship across all sectors and 273 per posting in tech¹¹. More telling than the aggregate: financial services firms that used to recruit from campus investment and trading societies have stopped, with students relaying that companies "now rely on AI to do intern-level tasks"¹¹. The first task to be automated was the cheapest failure available.

The same substitution is visible from the executive suite. The Oliver Wyman Forum and NYSE surveyed 494 CFOs whose public companies represent roughly 12% of global market capitalization. Sixty-four percent expect their finance function to shift away from junior roles over the next three years. Only 13% expect a shift toward more junior roles, against 41% toward mid-level and 23% toward senior¹². Their own word for the shape they describe is a diamond, replacing the pyramid.

Then there is the number that keeps the argument honest: only 8% of those CFOs have deployed AI-assisted tools or agents at scale in finance. Between 70% and 81% are still in planning or pilot¹². Juniors are losing entry-level positions to an automation that, in finance, has barely shipped.

The economists have the causation story too, and it is not the flattering one. In June 2026, New York Fed economists estimated that remote work availability could explain 64% of the increase in unemployment among young college graduates, and that the young-old unemployment gap opened in remote-capable occupations even after controlling for AI exposure¹³. Their mechanism is not automation. It is that managers cannot teach someone they cannot watch.

Both forces point the same direction anyway. 

A task you can do without supervision is exactly a task an agent can do without supervision, and exactly a task that was never going to teach you anything.

Google's own DORA research reached the same conclusion from the vendor side, which is worth more for being against interest. Its 2025 report notes that as senior developers self-serve through AI, "junior engineers may lose opportunities to build foundational skills," and argues organizations must treat developer growth as seriously as throughput¹⁴. Nearly 5,000 professionals were surveyed. 90% use AI, 80% report productivity gains, and only 24% report trusting its output a lot or a great deal¹⁵.

That is the contradiction worth sitting with. The people using it most are the people who trust it least, and the industry has decided to measure adoption rather than judgment.

![The follow-up estimate is a speedup whose interval crosses zero, with a six-month gap where almost no data was collected because participants withdrew. Source: METR | Sources [16]](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HUHOnqvaAAE8lK7.png)

There is a reason the measurement failure is not an oversight. Every widely deployed productivity metric counts output. Throughput, pull requests, lines generated. None of them can register whether the person who produced the work could have produced it unaided. METR found the same asymmetry in the other direction: developers who used AI on their own repositories expected a 24% speedup, and after the experiment still believed they had been sped up, when the measured result was a 19% slowdown¹⁶.

Then in February 2026, METR stopped. The follow-up expanded to 57 developers, 143 repositories and over 800 tasks, and both confidence intervals crossed zero. The stated reasons are more revealing than the results: a growing share of developers refused to do 50% of their work without AI, even at $50 an hour, and 30% to 50% of them withheld tasks they did not want to perform without AI¹⁶.

You cannot run a randomized trial of a state that has exited the population.

The default assumption in every confident take on this question is that the work is hard and the tools are weak, and the interesting question is which of the two is improving. That framing is a distraction. 

The work is not uniformly hard. It is mostly a thin layer of genuinely hard reasoning sitting on top of a thick layer of tasks that teach you nothing about the hard part, and the thick layer is what juniors were given to do.

Software survived this before. COBOL did not die when the industry declared it dead. Reuters put the installed base at roughly 220 billion lines, with about 43% of banking systems and 95% of ATM transactions depending on it as of 2017¹⁷. The Micro Focus/Vanson Bourne survey that claims a far larger figure is vendor-commissioned and should be treated as marketing, but the direction is not in dispute: by 2026, TIOBE had COBOL oscillating between nineteenth and twenty-fourth place all year, with no trend decline¹⁸. The oldest language in that ranking never died. It just stopped being where new people started.

Which is the actual warning. Nothing about COBOL's survival suggests that junior software engineering is safe. It suggests something colder. 

When old code outlives the apprenticeship that used to regenerate the people who maintain it, the work does not disappear. It becomes debt, serviced by whoever is left, with nobody coming up behind them.

The question is whether anyone is still going to give them the thing that makes them good: an environment where being wrong is survivable and being wrong is frequent. For twenty years, junior software work was that environment, and nobody designed it that way on purpose. It was just what was left over after the senior work got finished, and it was cheap enough that nobody minded.

That is the thing now gone. The senior work got faster, so the leftover shrank.
